The Rock I Keep Walking Around

A response to “The Obstacle in Our Path,” and to the habit of complaining about problems instead of moving them.

## Introduction

When I first read “The Obstacle in Our Path,” I thought it was a simple story about a king, a boulder, and a farmer. The more I thought about it, the more it felt like a mirror. The merchants and officials in the story did not fail because the rock was too heavy. They failed because they decided it was somebody else’s problem. The farmer decided it was his. That small difference is what this reflection is about, and it is a difference I need to learn to make in my own life.

## Body
### The message

The central message of the story is that every obstacle is a chance to improve ourselves and help others, and that the reward belongs to those who choose action over complaint. What stayed with me is that the king never pretended the road was fair. The boulder was truly in the way, and the farmer was just as tired as everyone else who walked by. The story does not say that problems are small. It says that our response is a choice. Responsibility, perseverance, and selflessness stood out to me, especially because the farmer was not looking for a prize. He cleared the road because it needed clearing. The gold was a surprise, not the goal.

### My connection

This story connects to my life more than I like to admit. As a Grade 12 student, my boulders are deadlines, difficult subjects, and the pressure of deciding what comes after graduation. Too often, I have behaved like the merchants. I complain in the group chat that a project is too much. I scroll on my phone when I should be reviewing. I tell myself I will start tomorrow. Complaining feels like progress for a moment, the same way a reaction on a post feels like helping, but the work is still sitting in the middle of the road. I even wrote a short story of my own, “Beyond the Screen,” about a boy who finally puts down his phone to help a neighbor clear a clogged canal. While writing it, I realized I was writing about myself.

### What I realized

The character who inspired me most is the farmer, because he had every excuse to walk around the rock. He was carrying vegetables, he was probably tired, and he was not the one who put it there. Yet he set down his basket and pushed. That image taught me that courage is often quiet. It does not look like a speech. It looks like one person kneeling in the dirt and doing the work. I also noticed that his effort helped everyone who traveled that road after him, including people he would never meet. That made me think about how my own effort at school, at home, and in my community can become a gift to someone else, even when no one is watching.

## Conclusion

In the end, I learned that obstacles are not signs that I am on the wrong road. They are part of the road. The strengths I can rely on are patience, my habit of finishing what I start once I finally begin, and the support of my family. From now on, when I feel the urge to complain, I want to ask myself one question first: what is one thing I can do about this right now? I hope to become a person who moves the rock instead of walking around it, and who makes the road easier for the next person. If I could write one sentence to encourage my future self, it would be this:

// note to my future self

Do the hard thing first, and help someone along the way.
